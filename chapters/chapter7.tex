\chapter{Challenges, Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{chap:conclusion}
\chaptermark{Challenges, Conclusion and Future Work}

This chapter covers the difficulties encountered during the security work, the conclusions drawn from it, and the work that remains for later phases.

\section{Challenges}
\label{sec:conc-challenges}

\subsection{Technical Challenges}

\textbf{I started with no background and no map.} I had not built software before this project, so reading someone else's code and changing it safely were things I learned while doing them. Nor was there a structure to follow. At the beginning my learning was scattered, one topic, then another, in no order. What structure existed came later, from the frameworks in Chapter \ref{chap:met}; Section \ref{sec:conc-reflection} reflects further on how that gap closed.

\textbf{Security began after development.} A teammate wrote the application code first, because I was new to this area and the access rules were not ready, and the team could not wait. That was the right call for a six-month phase, but it set the method: I could not build access control from a written table, because the code already existed. I read the code, wrote down what it allowed each role to do, compared that against the workflow the team needed, and changed the code where they differed. A substantial number of rules did not match what the hospital wanted, and some expected actions did not exist at all.

\textbf{The domain was entirely new.} Electronic medical records, the hospital's own information system, the FHIR standard, and how insurance approval works were all unfamiliar. Before any of us could start building, we spent weeks understanding the process itself, what the hospital already had, what they wanted, and how people actually worked. Access control cannot be designed without understanding the workflow it protects, so learning the domain was a prerequisite rather than background reading.

\textbf{Evaluation used the time that implementation would have needed.} The team spent several months trialling Microsoft Entra ID before it was ruled out; Section \ref{sec:conc-reflection} describes the decision itself. Federation with the hospital's staff directory was delayed by the time spent on that trial. It is now complete, and Section \ref{sec:impl-iam} describes the result.

\textbf{Almost everything was designed by revision.} Access rules were built one at a time, decided, implemented, checked, and sometimes the requirement itself changed once people saw the result. The audit trail had no template at all: I decided what to record, revised it, took it to the IT head, revised again, researched, and revised once more. Verification behaved the same way, since each time I finished checking, a teammate found something that sent the work back round. The gaps recorded in Chapter \ref{chap:result} reflect a design arrived at by iteration rather than one worked out in advance.

\subsection{Stakeholder Challenges}

The workflow had to be understood before it could be secured. Who may do what depends on which step a case has reached, so the flow had to be settled before the rules enforcing it could be written. The flow follows the hospital's existing paper procedure, so I began with the paper, reading the forms, following how a file moved between departments, and learning the terms doctors and hospital staff use for what those forms contain. None of that vocabulary was familiar. The flow was not mine to settle either: the Coordinator and Finance teams had to agree to it between them, over a series of meetings, and each change to it changed the rules enforcing it. Section \ref{sec:met-design} describes the weekly cycle this ran through; what follows here is the difficulty of that process, not a separate account of it.

Even now, with the flow decided and built, it is not certain that it matches what those teams expect in daily use.

Some findings were questions rather than defects. Whether the FHIR server is needed at all is a decision for the hospital, not for me. Whether the Coordinator should keep any ability to change prices after Finance has confirmed them was also such a question; it was raised with the hospital's team and settled during this work, in favour of removing that ability rather than keeping it, described in Section \ref{sec:impl-iam}. Questions of this kind took longer to settle than code fixes. Changes of that kind also affected my teammates, locking a form or restricting an action alters what they are building against, so they were raised before being applied rather than afterwards.

\subsection{Personal and Organisational Reflection}
\label{sec:conc-reflection}

\paragraph{Individual}

On the first day of this placement, I knew almost nothing relevant to the work ahead: I could not read code, I was unfamiliar with the application's interface, the framework it was built on, or how a hospital actually operates, let alone the specific GOP workflow or the vocabulary staff use for it. What changed this was not becoming a confident programmer myself, but a steady sequence of real conversations, with Finance (Bong Kimheng), with doctors (Dr. Denny, Dr. Visoth, Dr. Justyna), with the Coordinator team (Mrs. Debbie, Nurse Riza), and with the IT team (Bong Udom, Bong Chantha, Bong Savda), alongside guidance from my supervisor. My teammate Rith's own work also shaped how I learned: he built the application's interface and workflow, and once that existed, I could adapt and build my own work against something real rather than something abstract. Data and how it was queried remained genuinely difficult for me; there were times my supervisor, Dr. Elias, would discuss it and I still did not fully follow. But seeing Rith's work running, the actual result, was often what let me understand what a ``dream'' we had discussed in a meeting actually looked like once it existed, more than the discussion itself ever could. I learned to direct AI tooling to investigate and implement, to check specific things myself where I could, such as configuration files for default credentials, and to verify what the tooling reported against the running system rather than accepting it on trust. That skill, knowing what to check, recognising a wrong answer, and taking responsibility for the final decision, is what actually grew over six months, more than fluency in writing code by hand.

Confidence was something I had to build, not something I started with. On my first days, I was genuinely afraid, across every team I worked with, doctors, Finance, the Coordinator team, IT, my supervisor, and Dr. Ebner, Intercare's Deputy CEO, alike, that I would use the wrong word, explain something poorly, or come across as rude simply by not knowing how to phrase a question. That fear turned out to be unfounded. Rather than judging what I did not yet know, everyone who contributed and participated in this project treated me and my teammates as part of the group, almost like younger siblings, and created an environment where I could ask questions and share half-formed ideas honestly, without it ever tipping into disrespect. That sense of closeness, being able to speak freely while still being taken seriously, is what actually let the weekly stakeholder conversations described in Section \ref{sec:met-design} work. I would not have been able to ask any of the teams, doctors, Finance, the Coordinator team, or IT, to correct my assumptions about the workflow, or to speak plainly with my supervisor. Much of that confidence came directly from all of their support.

\paragraph{Team}

Within the three-person team, this growth did not start smoothly. Rith, Chamrath, and I were not initially close, and there was an early period of awkwardness where we did not yet know how to talk to one another comfortably. Rith was the one who consistently kept the team connected and made the work feel less tense. On the security side specifically, I started from genuinely knowing nothing, not even how to build an RBAC and ABAC table, and my first attempt to bring the team into that work was to have AI generate a document and simply share the file for them to read. That did not work: neither Chamrath nor Rith had the background to make sense of it on their own, and without a shared conversation behind it, the team pulled in different directions rather than together, leading to some real disagreement and misunderstanding.

What changed this was replacing the file-sharing with direct conversation. Instead of sending something off for the team to interpret alone, I started talking through the access-control questions with them directly, explaining what I was trying to figure out and asking what would actually work for how they were building the application. That shift took real effort at first. I did not have the words ready, and these conversations took time. But over many repeated discussions, the team reached a point where we understood what each person needed and what a workable outcome looked like for all three of us, including ways of expressing an idea that I could not have put into words earlier in the placement. From that point on, the communication, whether in person, by chat, or by message, became smooth, and my security questions stopped being something delivered to the team and became something worked out with them.

My contribution to the team's design conversations often took the shape of a ``what if'' question, which I would raise and the team would then discuss together. Two access-control rules described in Chapter \ref{chap:impl} came about this way. The first concerned when a doctor's ability to edit a case should end: the original build let a doctor keep editing indefinitely, and I raised the question of what if that ability ended once the case reached Finance's queue, since Finance was meant to be working from a figure the doctors had already committed to. The second concerned what happened when a case was picked up: originally, once an anaesthetist opened an unassigned case it became permanently theirs, with no way to release it back. I raised the same kind of question there too, what if a doctor could hand a case back rather than being stuck with it. Both questions led to real discussion with the team about what should happen, and both are now working rules in the deployed system.

Not every hard choice in this project was about making something secure versus easy for a doctor or Finance to use. Some were about making something secure versus easy for our own team to build. The clearest example was deciding how users would sign in. The original plan was Entra ID, so staff could use the same Microsoft login they already had, without needing to remember another password. When that plan fell through, we first considered building a simple login inside the application itself, since that would have been the easiest for us to set up. But we decided against it: staff would have needed yet another separate password just for this one system, and it was less secure than the alternatives, along with a few other concerns the team raised. I went back and researched further, and found Keycloak. It fit what we actually needed, but it came with a real cost: none of us had used it before, and it took much more setup work than the simpler option would have. After more discussion, the team agreed it was worth the extra effort, and that decision became the login system described in Chapter \ref{chap:impl}.

\paragraph{Organisation}

Intercare gained more than a working application from this project. Before this work, the rules governing who could do what existed only as unwritten habit in the paper process itself, understood by whoever had always done that part of the job, and once the system was built, that same logic lived scattered inside the code, understood only by whoever had written it. It now exists as a reviewed access control rules document, built and revised with the team over several rounds, that anyone can check without reading the code or asking the person who wrote it. The hospital also now has a real audit trail, a tamper-proof record of who did what and when, which did not exist before this work at all, on paper or otherwise. And rather than leaving open issues implicit or undocumented, the future work described in Section \ref{sec:conc-future} lists exactly what remains, with a priority and an owner against each item, so the hospital's IT team knows what is still open without having to rediscover it themselves.

More broadly, this project was also where the hospital, and the team, learned what it actually takes to turn a rule that existed only as paper habit into a rule the software enforces. On paper, the Coordinator's role in the workflow, or the point at which a doctor should stop being able to edit a case, were things everyone simply understood and followed by habit, never written down as a rule anywhere. Turning each of those into a software rule meant making every implicit assumption explicit, deciding exactly when it should apply, and testing that the system actually behaved that way. That process, more than any single technical control, is what this project leaves behind for Intercare to build on.

\paragraph{Wider Practice}

If I had to give advice to another student team starting a similar project, two things stand out. The first is that communication matters more than almost anything else. Most of the delay in this project did not come from not knowing the workflow or the technical background. It came from things I did not dare to say to the other party.

When I first realised I did not know how to read code, I kept that to myself instead of telling my supervisor, Dr. Elias, directly, and carried that self-doubt for months before finally admitting it. His response was not what I feared; instead, he worked hard with me to find a way forward. When I did not know where to start on the workflow, I hesitated to ask, held back by wanting things to be perfect before I even began. It was my mentor, Loukru Makara, who told me to start first and improve along the way, rather than waiting until I felt ready. I also reached out to Bong Udom, Bong Chantha, and Bong Savda, who helped me find at least a starting point, even a small one. With my own teammates, when something needed their feedback or a decision, I sometimes avoided raising it, and the work turned into something I was quietly handling by myself rather than something we solved together. Once I actually raised it with them, they simply took on their part of the responsibility, because it was theirs to carry too. Speaking first and letting the conversation follow worked far better than trying to have the whole answer worked out before saying anything.

The second is to know your limit and accept it honestly. If you only really know A and B, admitting that is enough; there is no need to also claim you know C, or to tell people you can do everything, just to avoid looking less capable. Pretending removes the pressure to actually learn what you are missing, and it closes off the space you need to grow. Someone who never honestly admits their own limit and their own worth never really grows past it. Accepting where you actually stand is what makes it possible to ask for help, learn the next thing, and genuinely improve, rather than staying stuck pretending you already have. If there is one thing I would say underneath both of these, it is: do not run for perfection, run for progress.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conc-conclusion}

This project secured a system that automates the Guarantee of Payment process at a Cambodian hospital. An identity provider was deployed on the hospital's own servers, access control was built across forty-two defined actions for five roles, an append-only audit trail was added, and the result was checked by two methods before deployment.

The clearest result came from comparing those methods. Static analysis applied 257 rules across 356 files and returned fifteen findings, none about access control. Role-by-role testing of the same system in the same period produced forty-seven findings, twenty-three of them access control. The scanner was not badly configured: access rules here come from how the hospital divides responsibility, not from anything visible in the shape of the code, and a tool cannot check a rule it was never given.

The second result concerns what those rules depend on. A permission is decided by three things together, the user's role, whether the case is theirs, and how far the case has progressed. The third is easiest to miss, because it changes the answer for a user whose role and assignment have not changed: a surgeon becomes read-only when the anaesthetist finishes and the case moves to Finance. That step is what enforces in software the separation the hospital already practised on paper.

The limits are stated rather than implied. There is no automated test suite, so verification was manual and happened once. Several controls a mature deployment would need remain incomplete rather than partially built: testing under heavy load, multi-factor authentication, encryption on two internal connections, alerting on the audit and activity logs, and a data-retention policy, each recorded with an owner in Section \ref{sec:conc-future}. The system was served without encryption at the time of assessment, and two of its internal connections still are. Everything reported in this thesis is self-assessment, by the person who built the controls; an independent penetration test has since been carried out by Canadia Bank (IT security department) and the hospital's IT Security Division, but its findings are confidential and not reproduced in this thesis, so this thesis cannot say whether it confirmed or contradicted what is reported here. And the final review found controls that had never run at all: a page-rule file the framework never loaded, and three background jobs nothing on the server started. Each has since taken a different path, superseded, given a working trigger, or retired outright, described in Section \ref{sec:impl-deployment}, but none of the three were fixed by the simple means first planned. That combination is the plainest evidence that a system checked once is not a system that stays checked.

A third result concerns where these rules actually came from. Writing them down correctly took more than applying a framework; it took sitting with Finance, the Coordinator team, doctors, and IT, week after week, until the team genuinely understood how responsibility moved between departments on paper, described in Section \ref{sec:met-design}. Only then could that understanding become a rule the code could enforce. Section \ref{sec:conc-reflection} reflects further on what this placement changed beyond the system itself, for me individually, for the team, and for the hospital.

What this project produced is therefore not a secure system but a documented one: rules that can be checked, a record of what was found, a list of what remains with an owner against each item, and an account of how organisational responsibility was translated into software in the first place.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:conc-future}

Twenty-three items remain. For a reader who wants only the highest operational priorities before the full table: complete internal TLS between Keycloak, the application, and Active Directory; move Keycloak out of development mode; close the System Administrator write-access gap on clinical and cost data; add multi-factor authentication; automate security scanning in the build pipeline; and introduce monitoring and alerting on the audit and activity logs. Some items are not mine to decide. Setting the audit retention period, or deciding whether the disk should be encrypted, are choices for the hospital. Some need another team, such as the server administrator or Canadia Bank (IT security department), since they sit outside the application itself. The rest were within reach but ran out of time, and are left for the next phase.
